BALLOT MEASURE #2026-B
Econ 238 Environmental Bargain Proposal

Marine Protection & Clean Energy Corridor Act

Official Ballot Question (Max 75 Words) Mimic State Proposition Language

"Shall the state authorize a targeted, highly regulated underground transmission corridor for offshore wind energy through a strictly limited 2-mile segment of the coastal marine sanctuary, provided that the developer pays a mandatory 15% gross-revenue ocean stewardship fee directly into a permanent state trust fund dedicated exclusively to buying out commercial fishing permits in overfished zones, restoring coastal wetlands, and establishing permanent no-take marine reserves?"

What Happens If Someone Votes YES?

Write this clearly enough that an observer could tell afterward whether the proposal was actually carried out.

A vote for yes will allow the state energy board to grant a restricted right-of way permit for sub-seabed cable trenching through a small isolated sector of the marine sanctuary under strict environmental oversight. This would also enact a legally binding trust fund which would require developers to pay a heavy 15% of total revenue "ocean stewardship fee", pooled into a permanent coastal restoration trust used to buy out commercial fishing permits in overfished zones, restore coastal wetlands, and establish strictly no-take marine reserves elsewhere. Compliance will be tracked via mandatory annual public ecological audits and financial disclosures confirming that all trust disbursements are channeled exclusively into wetland restoration and marine reserve acquisitions.

What's The Deal?

Max 150 Words Total
Environmental Benefit Bought
The "ocean stewardship fee" will bring in large sums of funding which can be fed back into preserving the local marine ecosystems. Additionally, the newly approved cabling will allow for offshore wind resources that were not previously available.
What We Are Giving Up
Temporary seabed disruption and minor, localized habitat disturbance along a narrow, 2-mile offshore construction corridor.
Who Benefits?
Regional marine ecosystems, endangered coastal species, recreational fishers, and the state’s long-term clean energy grid.
Who Bears The Costs?
Marine life and local habitats immediately adjacent to the trenching zone during the construction phase.
Who Pays?
Offshore wind energy developers via upfront permitting fees and a perpetual 15% gross-revenue extraction levy.
Physical & Financial Scale
A multi-million-dollar annual conservation endowment generating permanent funding for state coastal restoration.

The Best Case For YES

Max 150 words
Transitioning to clean energy is an urgent priority, but renewable infrastructure also requires physical space. By allowing a minor, highly mitigated localized footprint in a marine sanctuary, we unlock an unprecedented, self-sustaining financial engine for ocean conservation. Instead of relying on fluctuating state budgets or voluntary donations, this trade uses private green-energy capital to permanently fund large-scale marine reserves and wetland restoration that would otherwise never be financed. It is a pragmatic bargain that accelerates decarbonization while aggressively expanding ocean protection.

The Best Case For NO

Max 150 words
Compromising the integrity of protected marine sanctuaries—even partially—sets a dangerous legal and regulatory precedent for public lands and waters. Once we open protected marine zones to industrial energy corridors, future projects will demand similar exceptions, gradually eroding the legal shield protecting our most vulnerable waters. Furthermore, construction-phase trenching can cause irreversible harm to sensitive benthic communities, disrupting marine mammal migration patterns and local fish habitats. We should not trade permanent ecological protection for speculative financial offsets.

Where Is The Body Buried?

Max 100 Words

Every interesting proposal has something its advocates would rather not talk about.

The weakest assumption is that mitigation fees and restoration projects can fully offset the ecological damage caused by disturbing fragile seabed ecosystems. If the trenching alters local sediment flows or permanently destroys a vital fish spawning ground, the localized harm is immediate and irreversible, whereas the ecological benefits of the trust fund depend entirely on future political management and efficient fund allocation. What could go wrong? Construction overruns or legal loopholes could delay the funding stream while the physical environmental damage to the marine sanctuary has already been locked in.

Why Isn't This Already Happening?

Max 150 Words

Analyze incentives, property rights, interest groups, political institutions, diffuse benefits vs. concentrated costs, and behavioral biases.

Environmental advocacy groups are traditionally built around absolute preservation ("not a single square foot compromised"), making any trade-off involving industrial infrastructure in a protected area politically toxic to assemble. Meanwhile, energy developers fiercely resist heavy revenue-sharing levies, arguing that a 15% gross-revenue tax makes projects economically unviable compared to building elsewhere. Because the costs (localized disruption) are immediate and concentrated on conservationists' watchlists, while the benefits (widespread marine restoration) are diffuse and long-term, neither political coalition has an incentive to champion this compromise.
