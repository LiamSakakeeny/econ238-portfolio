The Street Without Cars: Earth Day, 1970, and the Bill Nobody Had Priced

On a spring Wednesday in 1970, twenty million Americans, among them the head of the union whose members built the nation's cars, turned worry about the environment into a political force.

A Street Without Cars

Start with a street.

Fifth Avenue, in the middle of Manhattan, is a street built for the engine. It carries taxis and buses and delivery trucks past department stores and office towers, and for most of its life it has belonged to whatever has wheels and somewhere to be.

On Wednesday, April 22, 1970, for two hours, it belonged to everyone else.

At the order of Mayor John Lindsay, the city closed Fifth Avenue to traffic from Fourteenth Street up to Fifty-ninth, ending at the edge of Central Park. And into the space where the cars had been, New York poured. Estimates of the crowd on the avenue run as high as a quarter of a million people. The next morning's New York Times put a photograph of the avenue on its front page, and in it the roadway is filled with people.

Look at what they did with the pavement. Someone spread a picnic at the corner of Fifty-seventh Street, in an intersection where, on any other afternoon, a blanket would not have lasted a minute. Bunting in the city's colors, orange and blue, hung from the lampposts. Balloons printed with environmental slogans were handed out. A parade float rolled by carrying the Statue of Liberty in a gas mask, standing on a pedestal made of garbage.

At the southern end, Fourteenth Street was closed too, between Third and Seventh avenues, and became what the city's parks department would later call an ecological carnival. At its center was Union Square, where at least a hundred thousand people passed through over the course of the day, in one of the largest gatherings there since the socialist rallies of the 1930s. There were booths for different causes: cutting air pollution, controlling population, building cleaner cities. A fourth-grade class from a Manhattan convent school swept a corner of the park. The mayor spoke, along with the actors Paul Newman and Ali MacGraw. Lindsay also rode the subway that day, promoting what he saw as the transportation of the future, and told a television interviewer that the city was building twelve new subway lines.

And in City Hall Park, a Pace College student pulled on a gas mask that had belonged to his mother, who had worn it as a nurse in the Second World War, and leaned in to smell a magnolia blossom. An Associated Press photographer caught the moment. It became one of the images people still associate with that day.

Hold on to that picture, because it carries the whole puzzle. A wartime gas mask, and a flower. An accusation about the air, and the plain pleasure of a spring afternoon in a city that, for two hours, had been handed back to people on foot.

New York was one stage among thousands. Across the country, about twenty million Americans, roughly one in ten, took part in some twelve thousand events that April: rallies, teach-ins, cleanups, and lectures, in city squares and campus quads and school gymnasiums. Congress recessed early so that members could take part at home. The Today show gave over the entire week to the environment.

It is often called the largest organized demonstration in American history, and it was put together in well under a year by a handful of young people working out of a small Washington office.

So, three questions. Who made this happen? What did the people on that avenue actually want, given that the picnicker, the schoolchildren, the mayor, and the young man in his mother's gas mask may not have wanted quite the same thing? And what became of it afterward? Because the answer to that last question has economics in it, and it is a stranger story than the photographs suggest.

The Senator and the Student

The idea began with a senator who was lonely on the subject. Gaylord Nelson, a Democrat from Wisconsin, had spent years pressing environmental causes. Denis Hayes, who would later run the day, recalled that before it there was very little environmental activism to speak of.

Then, in January 1969, more than three million gallons of oil spilled into the Santa Barbara Channel off California. Nelson, by several accounts, was moved by the damage, and he did something characteristic of a politician looking for leverage. He borrowed a format from the antiwar movement. The teach-in had been invented on campuses to turn a protest into a lesson, with lectures and debate in place of a march. Nelson announced a national teach-in on the environment for the spring of 1970.

He recruited a Republican congressman, Pete McCloskey, to serve as co-chair, which made the effort bipartisan from the start. And he hired a young man in his mid-twenties, who had come to Washington from Harvard, to run it. That was Denis Hayes. Hayes had almost no money and a staff of volunteers, many of them students, who set up in an office and tried to coordinate events region by region. Then something happened that Nelson later described, more or less, as the day organizing itself. Local people took the idea and ran with it. By the time the day arrived it was less a single event than a template that thousands of communities filled in for themselves.

They chose a Wednesday because that was when the most students were on campus.

Now notice what they were tapping into. In 1965, the pollster George Gallup showed Americans a list of ten national problems and asked which three the government should work on. Reducing the pollution of air and water was chosen by seventeen percent, which put it ninth. Five years later, in a poll taken in late April 1970, just after Earth Day, it was chosen by fifty-three percent and ranked second, behind only crime. Other surveys, asking different questions, put pollution lower. One has it ranking fifth in the summer of 1970, behind peace and pocketbook worries. But every version shows the same thing: a subject that had been near the bottom of the list was suddenly near the top.

That is a country already changing its mind. Books had done some of the work. Rachel Carson's Silent Spring, in 1962, had taught a generation to wonder what was being sprayed on its food and fields. And the country was in a strange mood. The war in Vietnam was still going. Less than two weeks after Earth Day, National Guardsmen would shoot four students dead at Kent State. A generation of young people had spent the 1960s learning how to hold a protest and be taken seriously. Earth Day offered them a cause that did not seem to require choosing a side in the fights tearing the country apart.

Or so it seemed. It turned out to be more complicated, and the complications are the heart of the story.

A Very Wide Tent

Here is the first surprising fact about Earth Day: nearly everyone was invited, and nearly everyone had a reason to come. Politicians from the left, the right, and the center embraced it, at least at first. One commentator compared it to Mother's Day. Nobody in public life wanted to be against it.

But being for it meant very different things. Some came thinking about smokestacks and smog. Some came thinking about how many people the planet could hold, which is why one booth in Union Square was about population. Some came thinking about a city that could be walked, which is what Mayor Lindsay was pushing when he used the day to argue for fewer cars. And the Washington group that grew directly out of the teach-in, Environmental Action, announced in its founding statement that it would focus on poor people, African Americans, antiwar groups, and workers, as well as the congressional elections that fall.

Not everyone bought it.

On the right, suspicion arrived with a calendar. April 22 was the hundredth anniversary of Lenin's birth. Several Southern newspapers made ominous reference to the coincidence. The Daughters of the American Revolution, and a Georgia official then running for governor, called the whole thing a communist plot, and some conservative officials refused to sanction the day's events. The organizers said they picked the date for the convenience of students. A few commentators have argued since that it was no accident, but that is conjecture, not evidence.

On the left, and in Black communities, the suspicion ran in a different direction. For many civil rights activists, environmentalism looked like a distraction from the urgent problems of poverty, violence, and systemic oppression. At the University of Michigan's teach-in, the organizers ran directly into that skepticism from the Black community. Some inside the movement agreed, and tried to build a bridge. Civil rights activists involved in the Earth Day campaign argued that urban pollution fell hardest on poor and Black city residents, and urged campus environmentalists to reach out to the inner city. How far that bridge got is a separate question. But the argument was already there at the start.

And then there was the strangest ally of all.

A senator, a student, a congressman, a mayor: you would expect them. But years later Hayes would say that the biggest outside financial and operational supporter of the first Earth Day was the United Auto Workers. He put it bluntly: without the union, he said, the day would likely have flopped. We should treat that as his testimony rather than a settled count, but it fits what others have reported. The union gave money, and it turned out volunteers across the country. It printed and mailed Earth Day materials at its own expense, including publications that were critical of the polluting cars its own members built.

The Autoworker

Pause on that. The United Auto Workers. Its members assembled the automobile, which is to say, the very thing the picnickers on Fifth Avenue had just been spared for two hours. Why would that union underwrite the day?

The union's president was Walter Reuther, who had led it since 1946 and was one of the best-known labor leaders in the country, with a long record in the civil rights movement and the Democratic Party. Reuther had already been campaigning for cleaner vehicle emissions in the 1960s. In 1970 he endorsed the Earth Day demonstrations. And he pledged his union's support for air-quality legislation that the auto industry staunchly opposed.

Why? There are two plausible explanations, and the evidence points in somewhat different directions for each.

The first is conviction. Reuther had spent his life arguing that a union's business reached beyond the wage envelope, into housing and health care and the quality of the life workers led outside the plant gates. Polluted air in the neighborhoods where autoworkers lived was, on this reading, simply another thing the union was for fighting. People who knew him said he saw no contradiction between protecting workers and protecting the environment.

The second is strategy. Back the movement, and you get a say in what it demands. You keep a young, energized, politically restless crowd on your side. You help steer the blame for pollution toward management, and away from the people who work on the line. A union that ignored the environmental wave might find itself cast as the enemy of clean air, and a union that joined it could argue that protecting the environment and protecting jobs were not opposites.

I think the honest answer is both, and that this is not a dodge. A man can believe something and also see what believing it is worth. But notice that the strategic reading makes a prediction. It predicts that the union's enthusiasm should be tested when the costs of cleanup start landing on its members. Hold that thought.

Reuther was no saint to everyone. When he died, a Detroit group of Black autoworkers, the League of Revolutionary Black Workers, called him "a reactionary conservative labor leader." They said that conditions in the plants were worse in 1970 than when he took over the union, and that he had been an enemy of Black workers who rose up against racism and brutal production demands. So the same man was the environmental movement's most unlikely friend, and an adversary to some workers inside his own union. Real people are like that.

Then comes the date. On Saturday, May 9, 1970, seventeen days after Earth Day, Reuther was flying from Detroit to northern Michigan, to see the union's new family education center at Black Lake. It was a place he had championed as somewhere to bring young people for leadership training, and he and the architect had wanted it to show how to build without wrecking the landscape. He had been reelected to a thirteenth term as union president less than three weeks before. His chartered jet came down in rain and fog on the approach to the airfield at Pellston, Michigan, short of the runway. He was sixty-two. He and his wife May, and four others, were killed.

I want you to keep that date in mind. It means that Walter Reuther made his bet on the environment in the spring of 1970, and never had to see how it came out.

From a Crowd to a Threat

So did the street party actually do anything? The honest answer starts with an awkward fact. On New Year's Day 1970, nearly four months before Earth Day, President Richard Nixon signed the National Environmental Policy Act. In his State of the Union address that month, he had called clean air, clean water, and open spaces the birthright of every American. The wave was already rising before the crowd arrived. One historian, writing about Nixon's record, put the word "bandwagon" in the title, arguing that he signed the Clean Air Act not out of any great concern for the environment but because he saw where public opinion was heading.

What Earth Day did was change the arithmetic.

Environmental Action did something novel. It named twelve members of Congress with the worst environmental records, from both parties, and told voters to throw them out. They called them the Dirty Dozen. In November 1970, seven of the twelve lost their seats. The New York Times editorialized that a candidate's environmental record had become a new ingredient in American campaigns. The group would go on to defeat four of its targets in 1972 and eight in 1974.

Whether the campaign deserves all the credit for those defeats is the kind of thing historians argue about. Incumbents lose for many reasons, and the people who tell the story most vividly were the organizers. But what mattered was what other politicians believed. A vague sympathy had turned into a fear. And fear, in a legislature, moves faster than sympathy.

The laws came quickly. Nixon proposed the Environmental Protection Agency in July, and it began operating on December 2, 1970. On December 31, he signed the Clean Air Act. It had passed the Senate unanimously, and it drew exactly one dissenting vote in the House. At the signing, Nixon called the year a passage from the year of proposing to the year of action.

That is the story most people tell: a crowd, then a law. And it is not wrong. But look closely at what that law actually did, and what it did not do, and you begin to see the second half of the story.

The Bill Left for Later

Read the Clean Air Act of 1970 the way you would read a contract: for what it leaves out.

It told the federal government to set national air quality standards based on human health. A legal scholar writing a few years later observed that Congress designed those standards to protect public health regardless of how much it cost. It set hard deadlines, including for cars: a ninety percent cut in tailpipe emissions of hydrocarbons and carbon monoxide, mandated over the vehement objections of the auto industry. One of the senators who helped write the law, looking back in 2005, called the key idea technology forcing. You write the standard first, and the industry has to invent the machinery to meet it. And he recalled that the judgments about what technology was available, what it would cost, and how fast it could be done were political judgments, not bureaucratic ones.

The act also handed ordinary people a weapon. It contained the first federal citizen-suit provision, which let any individual or organization take a polluter to federal court. Legal commentators note that nearly every major federal environmental statute since has copied it. For a movement that had just learned how fragile political attention is, this was a way to make promises hold even after the politicians moved on.

Now bring in the economists. Economists have a plain way of saying what is wrong with pollution. A factory pays for its coal and its steel. It does not pay for its smoke. That cost lands on the neighbors, who never agreed to carry it. In 1920, the economist Arthur Pigou suggested a remedy: tax the smoke, so that the maker pays. In the late 1960s, a few economists, among them Dales, and Ayres and Kneese, were working out more ways to put a price on pollution, including fees and tradable permits. But as one leading historian of the field has put it, the early federal laws essentially ignored economics. Economists were mostly not at the table.

Congress chose instead what is called command and control: name the limit, set the deadline, specify the technology, enforce it. Why? We can only partly say. A commentator from the free-market side argues that Senator Muskie's committee looked at emission fees and set them aside. The economic historian adds a twist: established companies often welcomed command-and-control rules, because the strictest requirements fell on new plants, which kept newcomers out. And I would offer one more possibility, which we cannot prove from the record. In 1970, pollution was widely felt to be a wrong, something to forbid, and a fee can sound like a license to do the wrong thing for a price.

Whatever the reason, consider the consequence. A law that sets health goals and deadlines does not ask the coalition to say who pays. The bill is simply left to arrive later. And it arrived at the doors of the automakers.

In 1973, Muskie, the law's chief author, said that in 1970 the auto industry had claimed there was no way to develop the technology in time, and that since then it had run a national campaign in the executive branch, in Congress, and at the local level to change the law. By 1976, two members of the House, Democrat John Dingell of Michigan and Republican James Broyhill of North Carolina, were offering an amendment to postpone the car standards.

And the union that had helped pay for Earth Day was standing in the middle of it. Its 1970 convention passed a resolution acknowledging that environmental regulations could affect workers' jobs, and its leaders insisted that clean air and secure jobs were not mutually exclusive. But Reuther's successor, Leonard Woodcock, according to one critical account, spent more than two years arguing that the 1970 standards were too stringent, unnecessary for health, fuel-inefficient, and costly in jobs. Meanwhile another labor figure, Tony Mazzocchi of the Oil, Chemical and Atomic Workers, argued the opposite: that pollution controls create jobs in production and maintenance. So the labor movement itself split over the question Reuther had hoped to sidestep.

That is the test I asked you to hold on to. The coalition did not shatter. But the easy phrase, jobs and clean air together, turned out to be much harder to live than to say.

Did the standards work? Slowly, and unevenly. The auto industry itself later pointed to cuts of more than ninety percent in new-car hydrocarbon and carbon monoxide emissions, and more than three quarters in nitrogen oxides. But a critic noted that by 1988 Americans were driving 1.9 trillion miles a year, seventy-two percent more than in 1970. A rule that limits pollution per mile does not limit the miles. And it was not until the 1990 amendments that the Clean Air Act took up market-based incentives, including tradable emission permits, the kind of mechanism economists had been sketching since the late 1960s. It took twenty years for the cost-cutting idea to reach the law.

What the Day Was For

So what was Earth Day?

It was not the beginning of environmental concern. That was already surging, and a president had signed an environmental law before the first picnic was spread. Nor was it simply the cause of the laws that followed. The politicians were already looking for a bandwagon.

What the day did was show them its size. It turned diffuse worry into a visible constituency, one whose anger could be given names and addresses, and it managed that by being wide enough to hold a Republican congressman and a Wisconsin senator, a Manhattan mayor and student radicals, civil rights activists who doubted it, and the head of the autoworkers' union.

That breadth was its power, and it was its price. It was possible to assemble everyone because the day never asked anybody to say what clean air was worth, or who should pay for it. The law that followed inherited that silence. It named a goal and a deadline, and left the argument about cost to be fought plant by plant and amendment by amendment for the next two decades. Agreement on the goal lasted. When Gallup asked in 2000, eighty-three percent of Americans said they agreed with the goals of the environmental movement. But agreement about a goal is not agreement about a price. And a union that had stood on the right side of the first day found itself in the middle of the second.

Reuther never had to choose. He died seventeen days after the picnic on Fifth Avenue, on the way to a place he had built to teach young people how to lead. We cannot know whether his bet, that clean air and good jobs could be won together, would have held, or whether he would have ended up, like his successor, defending the car against a law his own union had helped inspire. History does not let us ask. What we can say is that it was a serious bet, made by a serious man, and that the country spent decades discovering how hard it was.

And the street? Lindsay closed Fifth Avenue to traffic again that summer, for four successive weekends, and then the avenue went back to the cars, as it remains today. But for two hours in April, a young man in his mother's wartime gas mask bent to smell a magnolia, and a picnic sat where a taxi should have been, and twenty million people made a point that politicians understood. The point was that millions of Americans would show up. What the day could not settle was the question that came after, the one that still hangs over every argument about the planet: once everyone agrees that something must be done, who pays?
